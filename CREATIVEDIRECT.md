Creative Direction Document
1. Emotional Journey
Arrival: calm confidence — stillness, one sentence, one real artifact; the visitor should feel they've entered a serious room, not a launch party. Sections 2–3: quiet recognition — "the record existed all along" should feel like a slow aperture opening, not a fireworks reveal. Section 4 (receipts): earned surprise — the click-through from claim to PR is the page's one "oh" moment; everything before it stays restrained so this lands. Section 5 (refusals): respect — the visual register here is almost austere; refusals presented with pride. Section 6 (inside the interview): desire — the before/after question is the emotional peak; give it the most typographic drama on the page. Sections 7–8: safety, then agency — warm, human, ending in "settle it yourself." The overall arc: from stillness to conviction, never from excitement to pitch.
2. Brand Personality
If 16Signals were a person: the staff engineer everyone trusts in a design review. Speaks last, speaks briefly, always points at the artifact. Says "I don't know" without flinching and is believed because of it. Never performs enthusiasm. Dry, precise, quietly warm toward the people being evaluated. Never salesy, never mystical, never impressed by itself. Test for every design decision: would this person wear it? Light rays: no. A well-set citation: yes.
3. Core Visual Metaphor
Evidence under examination. The entire brand is the visual grammar of a document being read carefully: the record (dense, real, timestamped), the annotation (highlight, margin note, underline), and the thread (claim connected to receipt). Every surface — homepage, product, decks, social — draws from these three moves. Nothing in the brand may depict emission, magic, brains, or automation. The brand does not glow. It marks.
4. Design Philosophy
* Show the artifact or don't show anything. Real report, real evidence structure, real citations. If a section can't be illustrated with something true, it gets typography and whitespace, not abstraction.
* Restraint is the trust signal. Every decorative element is a withdrawal from the trust account. Default answer to "should we add—": no.
* Specificity over atmosphere. A timestamp is more beautiful than a gradient. "March, p99 crossed 400ms" is the brand's idea of ornament.
* The candidate is a person, not throughput. Nothing in the visual system may render engineers as pipeline objects, funnel stages, or filtered rows.
* One idea per viewport. The narrative architecture demands it; the design enforces it.
5. Art Direction
* Product imagery: the report is the hero asset of the entire brand — photograph it like Apple photographs hardware: real content, real density, precise crops, generous negative space around it. Never fake numbers, never lorem, never blurred "dashboard suggestion."
* Abstraction: permitted only when derived from real data structures — timelines, citation threads, evidence strata. Abstraction must be traceable to the metaphor; if a viewer can't guess what it represents, it's decoration and it's cut.
* Illustration: none in v1. Illustration styles are the fastest way to look like a template.
* Photography: no stock humans, no whiteboards, no handshakes, no diverse-team-laughing-at-laptop. If humans appear later (customer stories), they are real, named, environmental, editorial.
6. Typography Philosophy
Two voices, strictly cast:
* Our voice — a serious grotesque sans (large, tight, confident) for narrative and claims. This is the company speaking: calm, declarative.
* The record's voice — monospace for everything that is evidence: citations, timestamps, artifact references, code, metadata, the "after" interview question's factual payload. Mono is the typographic equivalent of a receipt; the moment a reader sees mono, they should know this part is verifiable.
The interplay is the brand: sans makes a claim, mono proves it. Never use mono decoratively (no mono headlines for tech vibes — that's costume). Scale: fewer sizes, larger jumps; editorial hierarchy, not dashboard hierarchy. No gradient text, ever.
7. Motion Philosophy
One law: motion may only reveal evidence or connect a claim to it. If an animation does neither, it doesn't ship.
* The signature move — the receipt pull: a claim underlines, a hairline thread draws to the artifact, the artifact surfaces. Used in the hero, in the report walkthrough, in the product itself. This is 16Signals' equivalent of Linear's crisp transitions: a motion people associate with the company.
* Behavior: triggered, never ambient. Nothing loops. Nothing moves while the visitor is still. Scroll advances the examination; pausing pauses it.
* Timing: fast and precise (200–400ms), easing like a well-tuned IDE, not like a movie. Deliberate, never bouncy, never elastic — spring physics read as playful; we are not playful.
* The refusal state (Section 5) gets deliberately no motion: refusals are still. Stillness as integrity.
8. Color Psychology
Governing rule: color is semantic, never atmospheric. Every hue has a job; no hue exists for mood.
* Ground: keep the dark field — near-black with warmth (ink, not void). Dark communicates focus and technical seriousness, and makes evidence marks legible. But dark must be still — no gradient meshes, no glow.
* Ink: off-white for primary type. High contrast, paper-and-ink discipline.
* The evidence accent (one): a single hue reserved exclusively for evidence — citations, highlights, the thread, verified marks. Because it's scarce and consistent, the visitor's eye learns within one scroll: this color means "checkable." That learned association is worth more than any palette. The current teal must be interrogated: it's the AI-industry default accent and carries hype residue. Test candidates that feel more forensic than futuristic — a highlighter amber, an archival green. Whatever wins, it appears nowhere except on evidence.
* The coral headline: cut or demote. Coral is energetic, consumer, promotional — emotionally miscast for "the staff engineer everyone trusts." Headlines are ink. If a second accent survives at all, it marks human moments (the candidate section) and nothing else.
* Never: purple-to-cyan gradients, neon glow, sparkle iconography. That palette belongs to the category we refuse to be.
9. Component Language
* Cards are documents. Hairline borders (1px, low-contrast), minimal or no shadow — shadows imply floating; documents lie flat. Small radius (2–4px): precision, not friendliness. Every evidence card carries real metadata (source, date) as a design element — the "letterhead" of the brand.
* Buttons: quiet, rectangular, confident; primary CTA in ink-on-accent or accent-on-ink, no glow states, no gradient fills. Hover states are precise (hairline shifts), not decorative.
* Spacing: an unusually strict grid with generous vertical rhythm between sections (one idea per viewport) and honest density inside artifacts — the report should look pleasantly dense, because real evidence is dense and airy fake-UI reads as fake.
* Dividers and threads: the hairline is a first-class brand element — it is the citation made visible. Use it structurally everywhere; it quietly repeats the core metaphor.
10. Layout Philosophy
Editorial, not dashboard. Storytelling spine, artifact interruptions. The page reads like a serious essay — wide margins, single-column narrative measure, big quiet headlines — periodically interrupted by dense, real artifacts (the report, the evidence band, the before/after questions) presented like exhibits in a case. That contrast is the layout concept: spacious argument, dense proof. Asymmetry is allowed; chaos is not. Nothing tiles into a 3-column feature grid — grids of equal cards are the visual signature of "we couldn't decide what matters."
11. Things We Never Do
* Light rays, sunbursts, lens flares, glowing orbs, particle fields, gradient meshes
* Brains, robots, sparkles ✨, "magic" iconography, anything that anthropomorphizes AI
* Purple/cyan AI-startup palettes; gradient text
* Fake UI, invented metrics, lorem data, blurred screenshots
* Stock photography of humans; funnel/pipeline imagery that renders candidates as objects
* Node-graph hero backgrounds (cliché) and terminal/matrix aesthetics (costume)
* Badge walls, logo carousels before we've earned them, floating trust seals
* Ambient looping animation of any kind
* Mascots, playful blob illustration systems, confetti states
12. Inspiration (principles only, never surfaces)
* Stripe: diagrams as craft — invest disproportionately in the few explanatory graphics; they carry the argument.
* Linear: motion discipline and restraint as brand — nothing moves without purpose, and the product screenshot is the marketing.
* Vercel: semantic monospace and black/white confidence — proof that austerity reads as premium.
* Anthropic: calm editorial trust posture for a company selling judgment — the anti-hype register works commercially.
* Apple: one idea per screen, and photograph the artifact like it's the only object in the world.
* Non-tech reference worth studying: court exhibits and audit reports — the aesthetics of documents whose entire job is to be believed.
13. Creative Manifesto (the one-pager)
16Signals — How We Look and Why
We are the company that reads the work. Everything we make must feel like evidence under examination: a real record, carefully read, with every claim connected to its receipt.
We show artifacts, not atmosphere. If it isn't real, it isn't shown. We mark, we never glow. Our gesture is the annotation — the highlight, the underline, the thread from claim to proof. Nothing radiates. Nothing sparkles. Two voices: sans speaks, mono proves. When you see monospace, it's verifiable. One accent, one meaning: our accent color appears only on evidence. Scarcity teaches the eye what to trust. Motion reveals or connects — or it doesn't exist. Triggered, fast, precise. Nothing loops. Refusals hold still. Restraint is our trust signal. Every decoration spends credibility we're trying to earn. When unsure, remove. Dense proof, spacious argument. The narrative breathes; the evidence is dense; the contrast is the design. The candidate is a person. No funnels, no filtering imagery, no throughput metaphors — ever. The test for every screen: cover the logo. If this could be any AI startup, start over. If it could only be the company that shows its receipts — ship it.

Bottom line: kill the fan this week — it's not a weak choice, it's an on-brand choice for your competitors. Replace it with the annotated record and the receipt-pull, demote coral, put the teal on trial, and let stillness do the selling. The narrative says "built to be verified, not believed"; the design's job is to behave that way before a single word is read.
