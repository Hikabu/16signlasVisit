The v1 page had a structural flaw: its most persuasive sections were arguments, and arguments are copyable. Any AI hiring startup can paste "resumes are broken, AI changed everything" above their product tomorrow — many already have. What they cannot paste is architecture: the citation pipeline, the refusal state, the banned signals, candidate ownership, the self-test. So v2 follows one rule: every section must be a claim that would be a lie on a competitor's homepage.

Cut from v1, and why:
* Section 2 "The Broken Signal" (the resume/interview/take-home demolition) — cut entirely. It's the genre cliché, it's negative-first, and it's the single most copyable section on the page. Replaced by one calm, positive beat: the shift, not the wreckage.
* Section 7 "The Payoff" (75%, time saved, better interviews) — cut. Every SaaS page on earth claims a percentage and "better X." Replaced with your idea, expanded: showing what a single interview question becomes. That's a demonstration only a company with evidence-cited reports can make.
* Section 5 "How It Fits" (three-step workflow diagram) — cut as a standalone. Workflow diagrams are wallpaper. The consent mechanics move into the candidate-ownership section, where they're a worldview statement instead of a process graphic.

Section 1 — Hero: The Report, and One Sentence
Why it exists: The uncopyable page opens with the uncopyable artifact. Competitors show dashboards, scores, chat interfaces; nobody can show a report where claims visibly cite real pull requests, because nobody built the pipeline that makes those citations true.
Visitor question: "What is this?"
Core message: Software engineering changed. Hiring didn't. This is hiring built on the work itself. — paired with a visible, real evidence report.
Content: One reframe line (your formulation, kept: change-forward, not wreckage-backward), the report artifact with citation markers legible at a glance, dual CTA: "Explore a real report" / "Run it on your own work." No "AI-powered," no category label, no feature words.
Mental shift: "Another AI hiring tool" → "this reads actual work — and they're confident enough to show the output first."
Transition: "What do they mean, engineering changed?"

Section 2 — The Shift (One Beat, No Corpses)
Why it exists: The visitor needs the why now — but delivered as an observation about the new world, not an obituary for the old one. One section, one contrast, zero dwelling. This is the entire "market shift" allocation on the page.
Visitor question: "What changed?"
Core message: Engineering work became the most documented profession in history — every decision, review, and revision leaves a record — while hiring still evaluates engineers as if that record doesn't exist.
Content: The positive observation: a modern engineer's work is timestamped, peer-reviewed, versioned, and public or shareable — richer evidence than any profession has ever produced about itself. The single quiet contrast: hiring still asks that same engineer to perform on a whiteboard. No litany of broken things, no "AI changed everything," no statistics about application volume. One idea, stated once.
Mental shift: "Hiring is a mess" (tired) → "hiring is behind — the inputs upgraded and the process never noticed" (interesting).
Transition: "So what does that record actually contain?"

Section 3 — What Work Reveals
Why it exists: This is the retained heart of v1 (the evidence reframe), sharpened from abstract to concrete. It passes the copy test because it previews our signal taxonomy — competitors can say "we analyze GitHub"; they can't credibly show reading comprehension at this depth.
Visitor question: "What can you actually learn from someone's work?"
Core message: Years of real work reveal the things interviews can never reach: how someone decides, collaborates, recovers, and matures — witnessed by the peers who reviewed it.
Content: Three or four concrete revelations, each tied to a real artifact type: how they respond to review feedback (PR threads), how their architecture judgment evolved (the same system, two years apart), how they behave in incidents, how they work with AI. Emphasize socially witnessed — this is evidence other humans participated in, which is why it can't be faked in an afternoon. This section can quietly carry the "Sixteen Signals" taxonomy name, giving the company name its meaning on-page.
Mental shift: "GitHub analysis = counting green squares" → "this is closer to reading someone's professional biography, written by their collaborators."
Transition: "And you turn that into something I can use before an interview?"

Section 4 — Every Claim Has a Receipt
Why it exists: The product reveal, framed around the one interaction no competitor can demo honestly: click a claim, land on the pull request. This is the trust architecture rendered as UI, and it's the logo-removed fingerprint of the company.
Visitor question: "How do I know this isn't confident AI fiction?"
Core message: Nothing in a 16Signals report exists without a citation — every conclusion links to the real work it came from, so checking takes one click, not one leap of faith.
Content: The claim-to-evidence interaction, demonstrated live or animated: a statement about the candidate expanding into the actual PRs, reviews, and commits behind it. One supporting idea: the report opens with what was analyzed and what wasn't (the evidence manifest). Nothing else — no trust-principles essay, no security badges.
Mental shift: "AI summaries always sound right" → "I don't have to believe this; I can audit it in a minute — and so can my most skeptical engineer."
Transition: "Fine, but what happens when there isn't enough evidence?"

Section 5 — What We Refuse to Do
Why it exists: The most differentiated section on the page, and the one a competitor would never copy because it advertises limitations. Refusals are only credible from a company whose architecture enforces them. This section does triple duty: trust, ethics, and bias-objection handling — without a single compliance disclaimer.
Visitor question: "What's the catch — bias, gaming, judging people on their free time?"
Core message: The system says "insufficient evidence" instead of guessing, and there is a list of things it will never measure.
Content: Two refusals, stated flatly. One: a thin public GitHub produces no verdict — never a low score; absence of evidence is never evidence of absence. Two: the banned signals — no commit streaks, no volume counts, no nights-and-weekends activity; we score the quality of work, not the quantity of visible life. Optionally a third: no auto-reject exists in the product; there is no button for it. Present these as design decisions, proudly, not as legal hedging.
Mental shift: "This will punish my best engineer, who has an empty GitHub" → "a tool that admits what it can't know is the first one I'd actually let near my pipeline."
Transition: "Okay. So if I use this — what actually changes for me?"

Section 6 — Inside the Interview
Why it exists: Your idea, and it deserves to replace the metrics section entirely. "75% time saved" is a claim; a transformed interview question is a demonstration — and only a product that reads real work can generate the "after" question. This is the section where the founder-visitor feels the product in their own mouth.
Visitor question: "What does this change about how I actually hire?"
Core message: The interview stops being discovery and becomes depth — you walk in already knowing the work, and every question aims at a real decision the candidate actually made.
Content: The before/after question pair, exactly in your register:
* Before: "Tell me about a time you made an architecture decision."
* After: "In March you split the payments service after p99 latency crossed 400ms — walk me through why you chose that boundary."
One pair is enough; a second (e.g., a code-review-behavior probe) optional. Supporting idea, one line each: questions come from the report, targeted at its open uncertainties — the interview verifies what the evidence suggested. The time savings can live here as a quiet consequence in a subordinate clause, never as a hero stat. Nobody else on the market can show this section truthfully, because their questions don't come from the candidate's actual work.
Mental shift: "This saves screening time" (commodity) → "this makes my interviews smarter than my interviewers currently are" (desire).
Transition: "What does the candidate think about all this?"

Section 7 — The Candidate Holds the Keys
Why it exists: Every AI hiring company positions candidates as throughput. Only 16Signals can claim engineers as participants: consent-first, repo-level control, they see the report, they own the profile. This is the philosophy section wearing product clothes — and it's where the workflow mechanics from v1 now live, reframed as a principle instead of a diagram.
Visitor question: "Will good candidates refuse this?"
Core message: Nothing is analyzed without the engineer's consent; they choose what to share, see what you see, and their strong work speaks for them — most engineers have waited their whole career for that trade.
Content: The candidate's side: they select the repos, private work is analyzed without code ever being stored, they preview the report, and a strong evidence report means skipping a screening round — proving yourself once with work you're proud of, instead of again on a whiteboard. One structural note: there is no silent-scan mode; the product cannot analyze someone who hasn't said yes. This preempts the ethics objection and quietly states the worldview: the evidence belongs to the person who created it.
Mental shift: "Candidates will see this as surveillance and drop out of my funnel" → "my best candidates are exactly the ones who'd opt in — their work is their advantage."
Transition: "I'm convinced enough to be curious. Prove it."

Section 8 — Settle It on Someone You Know
Why it exists: Unchanged in intent from v1, because it was already the uncopyable close: the only hiring product whose accuracy a buyer can test against ground truth they personally possess. Kept as the sole CTA.
Visitor question: "How do I verify this in ten minutes?"
Core message: Run it on your own work, or an engineer whose ability you already know — then judge the report against reality.
Content: One CTA. One closing line that echoes the hero's reframe. Nothing else on the page below it.
